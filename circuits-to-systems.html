Physics & Systems Thinking

From Circuits to Systems: Bridging High School Physics and Systems Engineering

Conceptual Analysis of 11th-Grade Physics (Electricity & Circuits) through Systems Thinking and Optimization

By Amirali Zarei

Physics is often reduced to a set of rigid mathematical formulas meant solely for standardized exams. However, when viewed through the lens of Systems Thinking, the fundamental laws governing electrical circuits closely mirror the principles behind industrial processes, supply chain logistics, and organizational workflows. In this article, I re-examine core concepts from high school physics to construct analytical frameworks for understanding real-world systems.

01 Current, Voltage, and Signal Propagation: The Filled Hose Analogy

A common conceptual challenge in electromagnetism is understanding how an electric signal travels near the speed of light while electron drift velocity remains remarkably slow.

The most intuitive physical model is a water hose pre-filled with water:

  • When a hose is already filled, applying pressure at one end instantly causes water to spill out from the opposite end. One does not need to wait for a single water molecule to travel the full length of the hose.
  • The Voltage Source (Battery): Functions as the pump, creating the necessary potential difference (ΔV) to drive movement.
  • Electric Current (I): Represents the systemic flow rate in response to that initial pressure gradient.

02 Resistance as a Control Mechanism

V = IR

Far from being merely an undesirable source of energy loss, resistance (R) is a fundamental tool for intelligent flow distribution.

Electric current inherently follows the path of least resistance. In a multi-branch parallel circuit, we utilize Ohm's Law to regulate resource distribution:

  • Practical Demonstration: Consider a circuit with three parallel branches, each containing a resistor of a different value (high, medium, and low) connected to identical light bulbs. Upon closing the switch, the bulbs illuminate at varying brightness levels (dim, moderate, and bright).
Systems Takeaway: Resistance allows engineers to dictate how much resource (current) each subsystem receives, preventing overload and ensuring operational stability.

03 Series vs. Parallel Architecture: Designing for Resilience

The structural distinction between series and parallel circuits provides a crucial lesson in engineering reliability:

  • Series Circuits (Single Point of Failure): If one component fails, the entire loop breaks. This is analogous to a rigid process reliant solely on a single strategy with no fallback options.
  • Parallel Circuits (Redundancy & Plan B): In a parallel architecture, the failure of one branch does not halt the entire system. Modern engineering design relies heavily on parallel structures to ensure continuity when individual components fail.

04 Energy Dissipation and Joule's Law

Ploss = I²R  ·  Pout = εI − I²R

In any real-world system, energy loss cannot be reduced to absolute zero; however, it can be minimized.

In industrial and organizational workflows, "resistance" translates to bureaucratic friction, redundant intermediaries, and operational bottlenecks.

Optimization Strategy: By removing unnecessary intermediaries and leveraging modern technology — such as integrating Artificial Intelligence for repetitive computational tasks — we reduce systemic friction (R) and maximize overall efficiency (Pout).

05 Kirchhoff's Current Law: Conservation and System Equilibrium

Σ Iin = Σ Iout

This is an expression of conservation within a closed system:

  • Performance Balance: High-quality systemic output cannot exist without proportional input. The value generated by a system is a direct function of the energy, effort, and resources injected into it.
  • Process Management: In logistics nodes or urban traffic networks, an imbalance between incoming and outgoing flow leads to systemic congestion or stagnation.

06 Capacitors as Systemic Buffers

Capacitors store electrical energy in an electric field and release it when fluctuations occur, stabilizing the voltage across a circuit.

Supply Chain Equivalent: In logistics and supply chain management, Safety Stock (Buffer Inventory) performs the exact function of a capacitor. Maintaining critical buffer stock absorbs sudden demand surges or supply chain disruptions, preventing total operational shutdown.

— Conclusion

Physics is not merely the mathematics of matter; it is the modeling language of complex networks. A rigorous conceptual understanding of voltage, resistance, conservation laws, and capacitive buffering provides a strong analytical foundation for solving complex problems in Industrial Engineering, Logistics, and Systems Optimization.
